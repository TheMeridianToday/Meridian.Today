March 14, 2026 · The War Enters Its Third Week
Kharg Island struck.
Oil infrastructure threatened next.
A second chokepoint announced.
The United States bombed military targets on Iran's Kharg Island overnight — the single facility that handles 90% of Iran's crude oil exports. Trump explicitly threatened to destroy the oil infrastructure next if Iran does not allow free passage through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran answered by threatening to reduce US-linked oil facilities across the Gulf to "a pile of ashes." Hours later, Houthi senior officials announced "Hour Zero" — a threat to close the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the Red Sea chokepoint. Two chokepoints. Simultaneously. The cascade the Global South feared most is now visible on the horizon.
Kharg Island · The War's Most Consequential Strike
US Military · March 14 · Iran's Economic Heartbeat
90% of Iran's oil
loads here. The US
struck it overnight.

In the early hours of Saturday, US forces struck military installations on Kharg Island — a five-mile coral island in the northern Persian Gulf that handles approximately 90% of Iran's total crude oil exports, processing around 950 million barrels per year. More than 15 explosions were reported on the island. Targets confirmed by Iran's own Fars News Agency included air defences, the Joshen Sea Naval Base, an airport control tower, and a helicopter hangar. Fars reported that no oil infrastructure on Kharg was damaged.

Trump announced the strike on Truth Social: "We have totally obliterated every military target on Kharg Island. One of the most powerful bombing raids in the History of the Middle East." He confirmed he chose, for now, not to destroy the oil infrastructure. "However, should Iran, or anyone else, do anything to interfere with the Free and Safe Passage of Ships through the Strait of Hormuz, I will immediately reconsider this decision."

Iran's answer was immediate. Its military stated that if US forces strike Kharg's oil infrastructure, US-linked oil facilities across the Gulf region would be reduced to "a pile of ashes." The Iranian Parliament Speaker had previously warned that Iran would "abandon all restraint" if any Persian Gulf islands were attacked. Iranian lawmakers said the strike made ceasefire talks significantly more difficult.

What Kharg Is — And Why The Globe Held Its Breath
Kharg Island loads approximately 7 million barrels per day with 30 million barrels of storage capacity. Around 80% of its shipments go to China. If Kharg's oil infrastructure is destroyed, Iran's oil exports effectively stop — a move that would simultaneously remove Iranian leverage over prices and devastate China's supply position. A retired US Army brigadier general told CNN the strike escalated the war from "take out the military" to potentially "take out the economic lifeblood" of the country. A former White House Situation Room chief said Trump is holding the island "hostage" to force Hormuz open. Pakistan's Zohaib Ahmed of Synorix told The National: "Expectations of surrender or unrestricted maritime passage are unrealistic. From Iran's perspective, this conflict is framed as a war of survival." Source: The National · Al Jazeera · CNN · Dawn
The Second Chokepoint · Hour Zero
Houthis · Bab al-Mandab · Red Sea · March 14
Hormuz is closed.
The Houthis just announced
"Hour Zero."

Senior Houthi officials announced Saturday that their movement has decided to militarily align with Iran and will soon declare what they called "Hour Zero" — language widely interpreted as a threat to close the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the chokepoint connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. It is the entry point for all shipping passing through the Suez Canal. If Bab al-Mandab closes alongside Hormuz, there is no viable shipping corridor from the Indo-Pacific to Europe and the Americas.

The Houthis had previously held back, constrained by a 2025 ceasefire with the United States in Yemen. Dawn's Pakistan-based national security correspondent reported that the Kharg Island strike appears to have changed that calculus. The Houthis framed the Hormuz disruption as "political and emotional solidarity" — but Hour Zero would be operational. Dawn noted the announcement "hinted at the possible closure of another maritime chokepoint," which "would compound the impact of disruptions already affecting the Strait of Hormuz."

For the Global South: the Cape of Good Hope reroute that is already adding approximately $1 million per voyage is available as long as Bab al-Mandab remains open. If it closes, there is no viable reroute at commercial scale. Every major shipping lane connecting the producing world to the consuming world would be disrupted simultaneously. Source: Dawn.com · Al Jazeera

The Two-Chokepoint Scenario · What It Means
Hormuz: 20% of global oil and gas. Bab al-Mandab: 12% of global trade, all Suez Canal traffic. Together they represent the single most consequential pair of maritime chokepoints on Earth. The Global South economies most exposed are those that import energy AND depend on export access through both routes simultaneously: Bangladesh (garment exports + LNG imports), Sri Lanka, Pakistan, East Africa, the Caribbean. If both close, Cape rerouting becomes the only option for all global shipping — at all-time high VLCC rates already at $423K/day. The cost does not stay in the shipping companies. It passes through immediately to the end consumer. That consumer, in every case that matters to Meridian, has zero buffer left.
The Gulf · Strikes Spread Across Every State
Regional Strikes · UAE · Kuwait · Iraq · Lebanon
Fujairah burning.
Kuwait base hit.
Baghdad embassy struck.

Hours after the Kharg Island strike, smoke rose from the direction of a major energy installation in Fujairah — the UAE's east coast oil export hub, which routes crude outside the Persian Gulf entirely. AFP photographers captured images of the smoke. The Fujairah installation handles oil loaded via pipeline from Abu Dhabi, designed precisely as a Hormuz bypass. If the Hormuz bypass itself is targeted, the Gulf's only alternative export route is gone.

Kuwait's Defence Ministry confirmed that two missiles struck the perimeter of Ahmad Al-Jaber Air Base — one of the country's key installations housing US forces — wounding three Kuwaiti soldiers. Air defences detected seven missiles and drones over the past 24 hours; three were destroyed, two crashed outside the threat zone. Separately, Iraq confirmed a missile struck the helipad at the US Embassy in Baghdad. Iran's IRGC formally notified the UAE that US "hideouts" in the region are now "legitimate targets" following the Kharg strike.

In Israel, explosions struck near a pro-government rally in Tehran attended by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, President Pezeshkian, and security chief Ali Larijani — senior Iranian officials visible in public on International Quds Day for the first time since the war began. One person was killed. Iran promised to deliver an "unforgettable lesson." CENTCOM data: 6,000 targets struck inside Iran, 90+ vessels destroyed or damaged since February 28.

Lebanon · Humanitarian · The Numbers That Keep Rising
Lebanon · Medical Workers · UNIFIL · Iran Civilian Toll

killed in one strike.

Israel struck a healthcare centre in Borj Qalaouiye in southern Lebanon, killing 12 medical staff — doctors, nurses, and paramedics. Lebanon's Health Ministry strongly condemned the attack. Separately, Israeli artillery shells struck the headquarters of a Nepalese peacekeeping battalion serving with UNIFIL in the town of Meiss el-Jabal. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun is now pushing for a one-month temporary ceasefire and has asked the US ambassador in Beirut to pressure Israel — proposing that Lebanon begin implementing full state control over all weapons in the country, including Hezbollah's. The total Lebanese death toll since March 2 has reached 773.

Iran's Health Ministry counts 1,444 killed and 18,551 injured by US-Israeli attacks since February 28 — victims ranging in age from eight months to 88 years old. Isfahan: a US-Israeli strike on the central Iranian city killed 15 people, according to Fars News Agency. Hezbollah leader Naim Qassem stated his group is prepared for a "long confrontation" and frames the conflict as defence against 15 months of unaddressed Israeli actions. More than 250 US organisations signed a letter calling on Congress to halt war funding, arguing the $11.3 billion spent in the first six days is diverting funds from domestic food benefits and urgent social needs.

Diplomacy · The Yuan Offer And The Exit Ramp
Ceasefire Track · China · Iran · Trump
Iran offered to open
Hormuz — if ships
pay in Chinese yuan.

A senior Iranian official told CNN that Tehran is considering allowing some vessels to pass through the Strait of Hormuz on one condition: cargo must be traded in Chinese yuan. The offer has not been formally presented. It has not been accepted. But its existence frames exactly what Iran's exit ramp looks like from Tehran's perspective — not a military surrender, but a restructuring of the global energy payment system away from the US dollar.

Trump separately said he believes US Navy escorts for tankers through Hormuz will happen "soon." He told Axios the war would end "soon" because there is "practically nothing left to target" in Iran — while US forces were simultaneously carrying out their most intense strikes yet on Kharg Island and central Tehran. Lebanese President Aoun is pushing for a one-month ceasefire independently. Iran's three formal ceasefire conditions — rights recognition, reparations, and binding future guarantees — remain officially on the table with no US response.

The Kharg Island strike makes a ceasefire harder to reach, not easier. Iranian lawmakers said publicly that the Kharg attack had complicated negotiations. The IRGC's threat to destroy Gulf oil infrastructure if Kharg's oil terminals are hit means any accidental escalation — a single errant strike — now triggers a Gulf-wide energy infrastructure war. That is the environment in which the IMO emergency session convenes in London on March 18.

Meridian · Day 14 · March 14, 2026
Two chokepoints.
One ultimatum.
One yuan offer.

Kharg Island is the war's most consequential strike because it changed the nature of the threat. For thirteen days, the war destroyed military infrastructure. On Day 14, the US announced that 90% of Iran's oil export capacity is now within the scope of the threat. Iran answered that Gulf oil infrastructure broadly — not just Iranian — is now within the scope of its response. The war shifted from a conflict over Iran's military capacity to a direct contest over the global energy supply chain.

The Houthi "Hour Zero" is not confirmed and not yet operational. But Hormuz was also theoretical before February 28. The pattern of this war is that every threat eventually executes. If Bab al-Mandab closes, Egypt loses its remaining Suez revenue. Bangladesh loses its garment export route. East Africa loses its port access. The Caribbean faces a shipping shock compounded on top of a fuel shock on top of a food shock that hasn't landed yet.

Iran's yuan offer to reopen Hormuz is the most structurally significant diplomatic signal of the war. It is not a ceasefire — it is a proposal to restructure who controls the financial architecture of global energy trade. China, which buys 80% of Kharg's exports, which operates the China Lane through Hormuz, which has a ceasefire envoy in the region, and which is now being offered control of the payment system for Hormuz passage, is the most powerful non-combatant in this war. The exit runs through Beijing — and Beijing knows it.

Two weeks in. The Global South did not start this war. It is absorbing it at every level — fuel prices, food security, currency depreciation, shipping disruption, remittance compression, and now the threat that the two maritime corridors connecting it to the world economy may both close simultaneously.
