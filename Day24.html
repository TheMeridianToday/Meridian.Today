President Trump claimed "very good and productive" talks with Iran that could yield "a complete and total resolution." Iran's Foreign Ministry responded within hours: "No talks are underway." The contradiction leaves the five-day strike delay in diplomatic limbo. Israel struck Isfahan's explosives facility regardless, stating operations continue "according to unchanged plan." While Washington and Tehran dispute the existence of negotiations, Kenya faces fuel outlet closures, Nigeria absorbs 40% price surges, South Africa braces for record R8.64 diesel hikes, and the Caribbean implements piecemeal VAT caps without regional coordination. The Talk Gap · Diplomatic Confusion
US-Iran · March 24 · Contradictory Signals
"Productive talks"
vs. "No talks."
The five-day window blurs.

President Trump announced Monday that direct US-Iran talks were "ongoing right now," describing them as "VERY GOOD AND PRODUCTIVE" and capable of producing "A COMPLETE AND TOTAL RESOLUTION." He claimed Iran had made a "major energy-related concession" regarding Hormuz, calling it "a very big present, worth a tremendous amount of money" [^53^].

Iran's response was immediate and categorical. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei stated: "No talks are underway between Tehran and Washington" [^321^]. Iranian officials emphasized that the "concession" Trump referenced was not a bilateral agreement but the existing yuan-for-passage channel for Chinese-flagged vessels — operational since Day 14, not a new development [^53^].

The contradiction creates strategic ambiguity. If Trump is negotiating, Iran is not acknowledging it. If Iran is stalling, Trump is not admitting it. The five-day strike delay — now four days — continues, but the diplomatic foundation is contested. Israel has made its position clear: Brigadier General Effie Defrin announced that operations in Iran and Lebanon would continue "according to an unchanged plan, regardless of any diplomatic efforts" [^53^].

For the Global South, the confusion is costly. Markets that rallied Monday on Trump's optimism cooled Tuesday as Iran's denial spread. Brent remains above $113. The IEA's assessment stands: this is "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market." The 20% of global supply that transited Hormuz pre-war remains largely offline. Six vessels transited March 24 — up from the single digits of previous days, but negligible against the 138 daily average [^56^].

The Yuan-for-Passage Reality
The "concession" Trump claimed appears to be the China Lane — 12 vessels transiting Hormuz under yuan-clearance terms, first operational on Day 14. This is not a new US-Iran agreement but a continuation of the de facto arrangement where Chinese-flagged vessels pay yuan for passage while others face closure. India's Reliance Industries capitalized on the US sanctions waiver announced March 20, purchasing 5 million barrels of Iranian crude on March 24 — the first major Indian purchase since 2019 [^72^][^75^]. The payment mechanism remains unclear; Iran remains outside SWIFT. The lane is narrow, selective, and excludes most Global South importers. Source: Indian Express · Reuters · UANI shipping data
Kenya · The Supply Chain Breaks
East Africa · March 24 · Price Freeze Collapse
20% of outlets
already dry.
Dealers threaten halt.

Kenya's Petroleum Outlets Association of Kenya (POAK) issued an ultimatum Tuesday: lift the price freeze or fuel supply stops. Twenty percent of Kenya's 3,100 fuel outlets are already affected by shortages. The crisis stems from a regulatory decision on March 14 to freeze pump prices for 30 days despite rising global costs [^61^][^69^].

The arithmetic is brutal. EPRA maintained Nairobi prices at KES 178.28 for petrol and KES 166.54 for diesel until April 15. But these calculations used cargoes received February 10-March 9 — pre-war pricing. Diesel import costs already rose 8.46% January-February. The next review will incorporate the full war premium. Until then, dealers operate at a loss [^69^].

"If customers are to continue receiving fuel, prices must reflect the actual market cost," said POAK chairperson Leah Kimathi. "Over the past few weeks, we have been operating at a loss, with our working capital being depleted daily. This situation is no longer sustainable" [^69^].

The risk is diversion. Kenya's unregulated neighbors offer better returns. "There is a growing temptation to divert fuel to neighbouring countries that do not operate under controlled pricing regimes," Kimathi warned. If diversion begins, Kenya's domestic supply collapses entirely. The government has pledged subsidies but dealers cite past experience: "The government is often unable to refund subsidy claims on time" [^69^].

Nigeria · The 40% Shock
West Africa · March 24 · Deregulation Exposed
N1,400 per litre.
Four hikes in March.
Africa's largest increase.

Nigeria has recorded the largest fuel price increase in Africa since the war began: 39.5% between February 23 and March 16, according to Global Petrol Prices data [^59^]. By March 24, prices in Lagos reached N1,332 per litre at MRS stations, N1,330 at NNPC outlets, and N1,371 in Kaduna [^68^]. Some locations reported N1,400-1,500 per litre [^70^].

The Dangote Refinery — commissioned to stabilize domestic supply — has implemented four price hikes in March alone: from N774 to N875, then N995, N1,175, and now N1,245 per litre at the ex-depot gate [^60^]. The refinery's statement noted that despite the increases, "Nigeria has retained one of the lowest petrol prices globally" at $0.88 per litre, below the $1.32 global average [^60^].

The structural vulnerability is stark. Nigeria produces crude but imports refined products. The 2023 subsidy removal eliminated the government buffer. With a fully deregulated market, global shocks transmit instantly to domestic prices. "The truth is that we probably have seen the worst, but that will be dependent on a number of elements that continue to play out in Iran," said Femi Ladain of Argente Capital Partners [^70^].

The windfall trap looms. Nigerian crude has surged above $110 per barrel, exceeding the $64.85 budget benchmark. But production has fallen to 1.31 million barrels per day — below the 1.5 million OPEC quota. The revenue gain from price is offset by volume loss. And as Ladain noted, "Were the country producing above 2 million barrels per day, as it did more than a decade ago, it would be better positioned to benefit" [^70^].

"The universal fear is the effect the rise in prices is having on fuel, a staple commodity in every one of the countries for ordinary people as well as industries. In some cases, such as Ethiopia, the government has already introduced fuel subsidies to shield people from the impact."

South Africa · The April Catastrophe
Southern Africa · March 24 · Projection Warning
Diesel: +R8.64.
Petrol: +R5.20.
Worse than Ukraine 2022.

The Central Energy Fund's latest forecasts project the largest single-month fuel price increases in South African history. April 2026 estimates: 95-octane petrol up R5.20 per litre to R25.50; diesel up R8.64 per litre to R27.44. These figures include the 21 cents per litre tax hike announced in the February Budget [^65^][^67^][^74^].

The previous record was July 2022: R2.57 per litre following Russia's Ukraine invasion. The projected April hike doubles that [^77^]. The under-recovery — the gap between current prices and market reality — has reached staggering levels. At $120 per barrel, the petrol under-recovery jumps to R5.40 and diesel approaches R10 per litre [^63^].

The transmission to the real economy is already visible. Sasol shares have doubled from R100 in January to R214 in March — a 72% gain in March alone — as investors price in sustained high energy costs [^63^]. Empty diesel pumps have been reported across Gauteng, Free State, North West, Northern Cape, and Western Cape [^63^]. The Road Freight Association warns that transport companies must raise rates or face collapse [^63^].

The Reserve Bank offers no relief. Governor Lesetja Kganyago has confirmed rate cuts are "firmly off the table." The repo rate remains at 6.75%. Investec projects the April fuel shock alone will add 1.0% to CPI, pushing inflation toward 4.0% in Q2 2026 [^63^]. Morgan Stanley predicts rates on hold through November 2026 [^63^].

Global South · The Two-Speed Crisis
Regional Impact · Day 24 · Diverging Paths
Importing nations
absorb shock.
Producers capture windfall.

The Iran war has created a two-speed Global South. Oil-importing nations face cascading price shocks with no fiscal buffer. Oil-producing nations capture windfall revenues without coordinated deployment. The split is sharpest in Africa:

Importing economies: Kenya's price freeze has broken the supply chain — 20% of outlets dry, dealers threatening halt. Ethiopia has introduced emergency fuel subsidies. South Africa faces R8.64 diesel hikes that will add 1% to inflation. These countries absorb the full shock with depleted reserves and limited fiscal space [^61^][^63^][^69^].

Producing economies: Nigeria's crude trades at $112 — $47 above budget benchmark. But production shortfalls (1.31mbpd vs 1.5mbpd quota) and deregulated domestic market mean windfall does not cushion consumers. Angola, Ghana, and Senegal are early producers "some way off reaping benefits." The academic consensus from five scholars: "In the case of Nigeria, the danger is that any windfall that comes its way won't ease the economic burden faced by ordinary people" [^36^][^66^].

The bunkering boom reveals the rerouting reality. African coastal fuel hubs — Ghana, Namibia, Mauritius — see surging demand as vessels divert around the Cape of Good Hope. Bunkering volumes up 112% by early March. Mauritius bunker fuel sales nearly doubled in 2024; the trend accelerates. But this is transit revenue, not supply security. The ships refueling off Africa are not delivering to Africa [^61^].
