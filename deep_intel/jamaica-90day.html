Bottom Line
THE VERDICT

Jamaica's fiscal discipline is real and it matters. The island reduced its debt from 140% to below 50% of GDP over a decade. It has reserves. It has relationships with the IMF and World Bank. It won't collapse within 90 days. That has to be said plainly.

But here is the brutal truth: Jamaica built those buffers to absorb one shock at a time. What is coming in the next 90 days is not one shock. It is six simultaneous shocks — energy, food, tourism, remittances, trade balance, and industry — all triggered by a single geopolitical event that Jamaica had zero vote in and zero ability to influence.

The economic pain will not be distributed evenly. Electricity bills will hit the poor hardest — they spend the highest share of income on utilities. Food price spikes will create nutritional stress in households that were already stretched post-hurricane. Remittance reductions will hit rural communities that depend on diaspora money as primary income. The Jamaican middle class, the hospitality workers, the small vendors near Montego Bay hotels — they absorb the tourism shock directly.

The 90-day number that matters most: Real GDP that was projected to grow at +1.5–2.0% in 2026 is now tracking toward 0% to -1.5%. That's a swing of 2.5–3.5 percentage points — wiped out entirely by the downstream effects of a war Jamaica is not fighting, over resources Jamaica does not own, in a region Jamaica has no presence in.

This is what it looks like when small, open, import-dependent economies pay the cost of great power competition. The bill was not addressed to Kingston. It arrived anyway.
