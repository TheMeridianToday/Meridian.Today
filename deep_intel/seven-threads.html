01
The Real Mechanism
The Strait Was Not Closed by Iran.
It Was Closed by Insurers.
The distinction matters enormously — and almost no coverage is making it.
The dominant narrative is that Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz. This is not precisely what happened, and the difference is critical. Iran's IRGC issued radio warnings that any vessel attempting transit would be attacked. But Iran has no physical capability to seal a 21-mile waterway against the US Navy. What it can do — and did — is make the legal and financial risk of crossing high enough that the market closed itself.
The Actual Mechanism · Kpler Market Intelligence
"Insurance withdrawal is doing the work that physical blockade has not — the outcome for cargo flow is largely the same." Within 72 hours of the first strikes, Gard, Skuld, NorthStandard, the London P&I Club and the American Club all canceled war risk coverage for vessels in the Persian Gulf. Without war risk insurance, no bank will finance a voyage. No shipping company will risk a vessel. No captain will take the job. The market did what a military couldn't.

No buffer inventories. The freight cost is non-negotiable for them in a way it is not for larger economies.
02
Geopolitical Architecture
A Two-Tier Global Shipping System
Is Being Built in Real Time.
Ships are broadcasting "CHINA OWNER" to get through. The global maritime order is splintering.
Iran's government stated that no vessels belonging to the United States, Israel, European countries, or their allies would be allowed through Hormuz. It made no mention of China. What followed is one of the most significant and underreported developments of the week.
March 5 · Iron Maiden
A bulk carrier in the Persian Gulf changes its AIS destination signal from "For Orders" to "CHINA OWNER" and hugs the Omani coastline transiting the Strait. It passes safely. First confirmed passage in days.
March 5 · Reuters confirms
Three diplomatic sources tell Reuters that China is in active talks with Iran to secure safe passage for Chinese crude tankers and Qatari LNG vessels through the Strait. Beijing is pressing Tehran. Iran appears to be listening.
March 5 · Sugar trade splits
Mike McDougall, sugar trade veteran: Middle East sugar executives confirm "a few ships are transiting the Strait — most of them Chinese or Iranian-owned." Dubai sugar trader confirms some sugar cargoes passing while others are blocked — without giving criteria. A two-tier system is operating.
March 7 · Today · Sino Ocean
A second vessel — the Liberia-flagged Sino Ocean — broadcasts "CHINA OWNER_ALL CREW" and transits the Strait today. Picked up cargo from UAE's Mina Saqr port March 5. Two Chinese-flagged passages confirmed in 48 hours while everyone else is blocked.
CSIS Assessment
CSIS notes that even with Chinese assurances, dozens of Chinese ships remain trapped inside the Gulf because insurance markets still won't cover them. The deal exists but the mechanism is incomplete. China's diplomatic leverage has limits when the insurance architecture is collapsed.
What This Actually Means · The Structural Shift
The global shipping system has always been theoretically neutral — flag agnosticism, open seas doctrine, freedom of navigation. What is emerging in the Strait of Hormuz right now is a wartime exception that creates a permanent precedent. If Chinese-flagged ships get safe passage while Western ships don't, every future conflict involving Iran will have a built-in Chinese lane. Every commodity flowing through that lane will be priced differently from commodities blocked by it. The global South — which imports through all of these lanes — will experience this as a choice between more expensive Western-supply-chain goods and Chinese-mediated goods. That is a structural economic and geopolitical bifurcation happening in real time.
For the Caribbean specifically: the region's commodity imports — particularly fertilizer, wheat, and refined products — flow through Western-aligned shipping networks. If the Chinese lane becomes the only lane operating through Hormuz for weeks, Caribbean buyers will not have direct access. They will pay the Cape of Good Hope rerouting premium, or wait. Either way, it costs more and arrives later.
03
The Hidden Food Story
The Fertilizer Cascade.
This Is About 2027's Food, Not Just Today's Oil.
The media is covering the energy story. The agricultural story is bigger and slower — and the window to act is closing.
A third of globally traded urea passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Urea is the world's most widely used nitrogen fertilizer. Nitrogen fertilizer underpins approximately half of global food production. Without synthetic nitrogen, agricultural scientists estimate the Earth could feed only a fraction of its current population. The Strait of Hormuz does not just carry oil. It carries the feedstock for the food system.
Layer 1 · Direct shipment block
Persian Gulf countries — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE — are among the world's largest urea exporters. Their exports are stranded. Fertilizer prices rose 6.5% in February 2026 alone. Egyptian urea — the global benchmark — has risen 35% in one week. Northern Hemisphere planting season begins this month.
Layer 2 · The LNG-to-fertilizer link
India runs its domestic urea plants on LNG imported from the Persian Gulf. Qatar halted production at Ras Laffan — the world's largest LNG export facility — after Iranian strikes. So even countries trying to produce their own fertilizer from imported gas can no longer do so. The supply cut has two vectors simultaneously.
Layer 3 · Sulphur collapse
Sulphur — essential for phosphate fertilizer production — is largely a byproduct of oil and gas processing. Nearly half of global sulphur trade originates from Middle Eastern countries. If energy production stops, sulphur output falls simultaneously. The fertilizer supply shock is broader than nitrogen alone.
Layer 4 · Planting season timing
In the Northern Hemisphere, fertilizer purchases for spring planting accelerate in March–April. A disruption of weeks is serious. A disruption of months — if the war continues — means farmers plant with reduced inputs, reducing yields. That yield reduction appears in the harvest autumn 2026 and persists into 2027. The food price crisis from this conflict peaks not in March but in September–October 2026 and beyond.
Layer 5 · Sub-Saharan Africa and the Caribbean
In sub-Saharan Africa, fertilizer use is already structurally low. A further price rise reduces application further, cutting yields in a region already food insecure. The Caribbean imports virtually all its food. A global food price spike from reduced yields in 2026 and 2027 arrives as a direct cost increase for every Caribbean household — layered on top of the electricity cost spike from oil.
The Timing Paradox · Critically Underreported
The food story and the oil story are structurally different in timing. Oil price spikes hit within days — you pay more at the pump this week. Fertilizer disruption hits in 6–9 months — you pay more for food next winter. This means the food shock from the Iran war will arrive when the headlines have moved on and the political attention has dissipated. Governments will face a food price crisis in late 2026 without the political infrastructure to respond to it, because the connection to a war that may have ended by then will not be obvious to ordinary people.
Corn Belt farmers in the US may be forced to switch from nitrogen-intensive corn to soybeans — creating a major grain market imbalance. US Corn Belt → soy shift → corn supply drops → corn futures at record highs Q3–Q4 2026. The Caribbean, which imports corn for animal feed and food processing, absorbs this as a food cost multiplier entirely outside its control.

The 300 Trapped Tankers · A Hidden Supply Constraint
Around 300 oil tankers loaded cargo inside the Persian Gulf and cannot exit. They are physically stranded — full of oil, going nowhere. This creates an artificial supply crunch that is separate from the production cuts. Even if every OPEC+ producer maintains output, that oil cannot reach global markets while those tankers are trapped. The stranded tanker fleet represents approximately 600 million barrels of oil — roughly 6 days of global consumption — sitting immobile. When Hormuz eventually opens, there will be a release event that briefly softens prices, but that is weeks away.

The Alternative Route
Pakistan Called Saudi Arabia.
A New Oil Route Is Being Built Right Now.
The first real workaround is materialising — and it reveals who has leverage and who doesn't.
On March 4, Pakistan formally requested that Saudi Arabia reroute its oil supplies through the Red Sea port of Yanbu rather than through Hormuz. Saudi Arabia confirmed it was arranging at least one crude shipment via this alternative route and was actively considering wider diversion of its own crude exports. This is the first explicit Hormuz workaround confirmed in writing.
Why The Saudi Yanbu Route Matters — And Why It Can't Scale
Saudi Arabia has two export corridors: the Ras Tanura terminal on the Gulf (runs through Hormuz) and the Yanbu export terminal on the Red Sea (bypasses Hormuz via the East-West Pipeline). Maximum Yanbu pipeline capacity is approximately 5 million barrels per day. Saudi Arabia normally exports 6–7 million bpd total. The pipeline cannot replace Hormuz volume — it can only provide partial relief. And Yanbu exits into the Red Sea, which the Houthis are also attacking. Every tanker exiting Yanbu faces the same Houthi threat the Red Sea has been dealing with for months. The alternative route is real but it is not a solution. It is a pressure valve.
The deeper signal here is about leverage hierarchy. Pakistan could call Riyadh and get a supply arrangement. Smaller Caribbean and African importers do not have that call to make. The workaround routes that are materialising are being allocated to countries with the political weight to negotiate them. Countries without that weight — Haiti, Barbados, smaller CARICOM members — go to the back of the queue and pay spot market prices for whatever oil has been rerouted around them.

The Transmission Channel
The US Economy Is the Caribbean's
Second Oil Shock — And It Arrives Quietly.
Oil prices hit the Caribbean directly. But the US economic slowdown hits it a second time, through a different channel, with a delay.
The Caribbean's dependence on oil is well documented. What is less visible in the current coverage is the second channel through which the war reaches the region: the US economy. The US printed −92,000 jobs in February 2026 — the first negative jobs print since 2020. Goldman Sachs estimated that if oil remains at current levels, US consumer price inflation rises from 2.4% to 3% by year end. That puts the Fed in an impossible position: cut rates into inflation, or hold rates into a recession.
US Stagflation Confirmed
Oil above $90 + negative jobs print + Fed paralysed = stagflation. Stagflation is the worst outcome for Caribbean economies because it compresses both the channels they depend on simultaneously.
Channel 1 · Tourism Compression
US consumers with higher fuel bills, higher grocery bills, and job insecurity cut discretionary spending first. Caribbean vacations — costing $2,000–$5,000 per family — are discretionary spending. American Airlines, United, and JetBlue all report booking softness in leisure destinations when US consumer confidence drops. Jamaica, Bahamas, Barbados, Dominican Republic each have 40–60% GDP exposure to this channel.
Channel 2 · Remittance Compression
A stagflating US economy reduces the disposable income of diaspora workers who send money home. Jamaican diaspora in the US send ~65% of the island's $3.5B in annual remittances. If those workers face higher costs of living in the US, they send less. The effect is not immediate — it builds over months. By Month 4–6 of sustained US stagflation, remittance flows will start declining. That hits Caribbean household spending and government tax revenues simultaneously.
Channel 3 · Deportation Amplifier
Over 1 million Jamaicans are under active or potential deportation risk from the Trump administration. Mass deportation events would: increase the population of people needing services in Jamaica, reduce the number of workers sending remittances to Jamaica, and create political and social pressure on an already strained system. This is a third economic channel from the US that has nothing to do with oil prices.
The Combined Timing
Oil shock: immediate. Tourism softness: 1–3 months lag. Remittance compression: 2–4 months lag. Deportation impact: 3–6 months lag. These channels arrive in sequence over 6 months, each compounding the last. By September 2026, all three are simultaneously active. Caribbean governments planning budgets around pre-war assumptions will face a structurally different fiscal reality.

The Silence
CARICOM Has Said Nothing.
That Is Itself Intelligence.
The absence of a coordinated regional response is a data point. Here is what it reveals.
Eight days into the most significant global oil supply disruption since the 1970s — a disruption with specific, documented, quantified economic consequences for every CARICOM member state — there has been no coordinated CARICOM emergency response, no joint statement, no emergency session called, and no regional economic impact assessment published.
Why This Matters
In the 2022 food price crisis triggered by the Russia-Ukraine war, CARICOM convened emergency discussions within two weeks and produced a coordinated food security strategy. The Iran/oil shock is larger, faster-moving, and more directly relevant to Caribbean energy security. Yet no equivalent response exists. Three possible explanations — and all three are bad:

1. Capacity: Caribbean governments lack the economic intelligence infrastructure to understand what is happening quickly enough to respond in real time.

2. Dependency: The major response levers (oil import alternatives, strategic reserves, price controls) require fiscal capacity that most CARICOM states don't have at $90 Brent.

3. Alignment: Individual governments are waiting for the US or IDB to tell them what to do. That instruction has not come because Washington is focused entirely on the war itself, not on managing the downstream effects on small island states that nobody powerful is paying attention to.
What a coordinated CARICOM response could do right now, at zero fiscal cost: joint negotiation with Trinidad for emergency LNG supply at preferential rates, joint statement to attract IDB emergency fast-disbursing loans before the crisis deepens, shared economic intelligence on which sectors are most exposed, and coordinated communication to citizens about what to expect. None of this is happening. The window to do it at low cost is closing. The longer governments wait to act, the more expensive and reactive the response will need to be.
The Trinidad Opportunity · Specific and Concrete
Trinidad & Tobago is accumulating windfall revenue from LNG exports at precisely the moment its Caribbean neighbours are being squeezed by the same price spike. A Caribbean Energy Solidarity Framework — preferential LNG pricing for CARICOM members funded by T&T's windfall, paired with IDB credit lines — could partially offset the oil shock for the most exposed economies. This is not unprecedented: T&T has had such arrangements before under Petrocaribe-style frameworks. The political will to propose it has not materialised. Guyana is also in a position to contribute, with its Natural Resource Fund now growing rapidly. The money exists within the region to cushion this shock. The institutional architecture to deploy it does not.
