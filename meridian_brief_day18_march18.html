South Pars · The War's Most Consequential Strike Yet
Energy Infrastructure · Israel · US · March 18
South Pars struck.
70% of Iran's domestic
gas. Workers fled the fire.

Israeli air forces struck South Pars — Iran's massive offshore natural gas field in the Persian Gulf — on Wednesday morning, targeting phases three through six of its onshore processing facilities in Assaluyeh, Bushehr Province. Iranian state television reported that workers shut down the phases to stop spreading fires. The Tehran refinery, with a capacity of 220,000 barrels per day, was also damaged. South Pars supplies approximately 70% of Iran's domestic natural gas.

The field is the world's largest, shared between Iran and Qatar — Qatar's side is called North Field, and produces 18.5 billion cubic feet per day of gas that enables Qatar to supply roughly one-fifth of the world's LNG. Qatar's Foreign Ministry condemned the strikes immediately and forcefully. Spokesman Majed Al-Ansari called it "a dangerous and irresponsible step amid the current military escalation," warning of broader risks to regional stability and global supply. S&P Global Commodity Insights analyst Laurent Ruseckas said the strike would prompt a bullish stance for global LNG benchmarks JKM and TTF as Turkish buyers displaced by Iranian supply shortfall seek replacement cargoes, tightening the entire market.

Argus Media reported that further strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure could threaten up to 3.4 million barrels per day of crude output and around 1.5 million barrels per day of exports. Brent crude climbed as much as 6.3% to a high of $109.95 a barrel on Wednesday. European gas benchmark TTF surged 9.3%. This is the first time Iran's upstream oil and gas infrastructure has been directly targeted since the war began on February 28. Every prior strike was on military sites. The line has now been crossed.

Iran's Named Target List — Sent Wednesday
Iran's state television published Iran's formal response targets after the South Pars strike. The list includes: Saudi Aramco's Samref refinery in Jubail; Jubail's petrochemical complex; UAE's Al Hosn gas field; and Qatar's LNG facilities. Two large explosions were heard in Riyadh Wednesday night. Iran struck a province of Saudi Arabia where major oil fields are concentrated. If Iran executes this list, the Gulf's entire energy export infrastructure — Saudi, Emirati, and Qatari — enters the active strike zone simultaneously. That is not an oil price event. That is a restructuring of global energy supply for a decade. Source: CBC · Fortune · Al Jazeera
Assassinations · The Diplomats Are Being Killed
Leadership · March 17–18 · Iran's Negotiating Capacity
The man who could have
ended this war
was killed on Tuesday.

Israel confirmed on Tuesday that it killed Ali Larijani — secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council and one of the most influential figures in the Iranian system. Iranian state media confirmed his death on Wednesday. His son was also killed in the same strike. Iranian-American political analyst Trita Parsi, speaking to Democracy Now, described Larijani as "the consensus maker within the system — someone seen as much more open to a diplomatic solution." Parsi noted that Larijani had been in direct diplomatic contact with a key White House figure prior to the war, as recently as December 2025.

On the same day, Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz announced the killing of Iranian Intelligence Minister Esmaeil Khatib in an overnight airstrike. Iranian President Pezeshkian confirmed Khatib's death. Separately, Israel also killed General Gholam Reza Soleimani, head of the IRGC's all-volunteer Basij force. Three senior Iranian officials killed in 48 hours — including the one most associated with a potential diplomatic exit from this war. Parsi said the pattern raises the question of whether Israel is deliberately eliminating potential off-ramps for Trump by killing those who could engage in diplomacy.

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, speaking Sunday on CBS Face the Nation, made Tehran's position explicit: "No, we never asked for a ceasefire, and we have never asked even for negotiation. We are ready to defend ourselves as long as it takes." At Monday's foreign ministry press conference he added: "This war must end in a way that our enemies never again think about repeating such attacks." Iran's position: not surrender, not negotiation — deterrence.

"The Israelis have fought so hard to get the United States to go into a full-scale war with Iran for more than 20 years. It is in their interest to prolong this war as long as they can, and kill off any potential off-ramps that Trump may be looking for."
Trita Parsi · Iranian-American political analyst · Democracy Now · March 17, 2026
Nuclear · The Line Nobody Wants To Cross
Bushehr · IAEA · Nuclear Risk · March 18
A nuclear power plant
was struck today.
The IAEA said no damage.

The Bushehr nuclear power plant — Iran's only operational civilian nuclear reactor — was hit by a projectile on Wednesday. The IAEA received a report from Tehran and confirmed that no radiation was released and the plant suffered no structural damage. IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi reiterated his call for "maximum restraint during the conflict to prevent risk of a nuclear accident." Iran's nuclear regulator said the hit affected external parts of the complex.

The strike on Bushehr — even without damage — crossed a threshold. Nuclear facilities have been considered off-limits in modern warfare since the Cold War. The fact that it was hit and undamaged does not diminish the signal: the war is now operating in proximity to active nuclear infrastructure. Grossi has repeated his call for restraint multiple times in 18 days. No party has formally acknowledged it. The IAEA's statement carried no enforcement mechanism.

CENTCOM data as of Day 18: 6,000+ targets struck inside Iran. 90+ vessels destroyed or damaged. US forces dropped multiple 5,000-pound deep-penetrator bombs on Iranian anti-ship missile sites near Hormuz on Tuesday. The Hengaw Organization for Human Rights estimates 4,800+ members of Iranian military forces have been killed. Iran's military, for its part, has fired more than 500 ballistic and naval missiles and nearly 2,000 drones since February 28 — 40% at Israel, 60% at US regional targets.

Global South · The Cascade Accelerates
Regional Impact · All Regions · Day 18
Cuba is protesting.
Venezuela sanctions lifted.
None of it is enough.

Three weeks in, the downstream effects of the war are moving faster than any government response. The IEA's 32 member countries are releasing 400 million barrels over 120 days — beginning this week. Trump waived the Jones Act for 60 days to ease domestic shipping costs. Venezuela oil sanctions were eased by both US Treasury and EU. Targa Resources declared force majeure on LPG loadings from Houston. None of these measures address the Hormuz closure, the South Pars disruption, or the named Gulf infrastructure targets. They manage the edges. The core is unresolved.

Cuba — Protesters attacked a Communist Party office on Wednesday as Cuba's energy crisis deepened. Cuba imports the majority of its petroleum from Venezuela — whose own production is straining to meet US waiver demand — and relies on Russian fuel that is increasingly redirected. Cuba's power grid has been in rolling blackout for months; the war's supply shock is the final pressure on an already collapsed system. Democracy Now reported the protest as a direct downstream consequence of the regional oil disruption. Cuba did not appear in any Western war coverage this week.

Diplomacy · The Exit Ramp Gets Narrower
Ceasefire Track · Hormuz · Trump · March 18
Trump ignored
regional allies' calls.
Bloomberg: no deal, no Hormuz.

Reuters reported Wednesday that Trump has ignored multiple attempts by Middle Eastern allies to begin negotiations aimed at ending the war. Bloomberg published its most direct assessment yet: Hormuz will not reopen without a ceasefire, full stop. Iran's sporadic attacks on vessels and the threat of mines have put Tehran — not outside naval forces — in effective control of the flow. Lloyd's List estimated that even with naval escorts at full deployment, no more than 10% of lost volumes could realistically be restored.

Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One on Sunday that he is in talks with seven countries about helping to secure the strait, calling on them to send warships. "I'm demanding that these countries come in and protect their own territory," he said, without naming them. He also claimed Iran had asked for a ceasefire — which Iran's foreign minister flatly denied on the same day in the same news cycle.

The killing of Ali Larijani removes the Iranian official most associated with potential diplomatic engagement. Trita Parsi described him as the person who had been in contact with a White House figure as recently as December. The IMO emergency session is meeting today in London — the first of its kind for a single shipping route in the organisation's history. Its mandate is procedural. It cannot compel Iran to reopen Hormuz. Only a ceasefire can do that.

Meridian · Day 18 · March 18, 2026
The energy war
is no longer a threat.
It's the war itself.

For seventeen days, the strikes were on military sites. Nuclear facilities. Naval bases. Command infrastructure. The model was: degrade Iran's military capacity while keeping the global energy system structurally intact. On Day 18, that model ended. South Pars — 70% of Iran's domestic gas, shared infrastructure with Qatar's LNG that feeds one-fifth of global supply — is burning. The Tehran refinery is damaged. Iran has named its next targets by facility name: Samref, Jubail, Al Hosn, Ras Laffan. Two explosions were heard in Riyadh Wednesday night.

The man most capable of negotiating an exit from this war was killed on Tuesday. Ali Larijani's death removes the Iranian official who had been in contact with Washington as recently as December 2025. The intelligence minister is dead. The Basij commander is dead. The new Supreme Leader has not been seen on camera in 18 days. Iran is fighting a leadership-decapitated war with pre-programmed escalation doctrine and a named list of energy targets. The system is running on its own momentum now.

For the Global South: the IEA release, the Jones Act waiver, the Venezuela sanction easing, the Russia oil waiver — these are emergency measures designed to contain a price shock that is now secondary to a supply architecture shock. South Pars feeds Pakistan's LNG market through Turkey. It feeds Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. Its disruption tightens every LNG benchmark globally and every LNG cargo becomes more expensive for every buyer who isn't the United States. Cuba is already protesting. The Philippines has 50 days of reserves. Egypt's bond market is at risk. The fertilizer that should be in the ground for September's harvest is not.

Three weeks in. The war has become what it was always at risk of becoming: a direct contest for the architecture of global energy. The cascade runs faster than any government's ability to respond. The Global South did not start it. It absorbs it first and absorbs it longest.
