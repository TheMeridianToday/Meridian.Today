Day 8 · Key Developments
● Break · Dubai Airport
The Ceasefire Lasted Hours
Iran's president announced this morning it would stop striking neighbouring states unless attacks originated from there — framed as a de-escalation toward Gulf nations who had been caught in the crossfire. Within hours, Iranian drones struck Dubai International Airport. The world's busiest international aviation hub, handling 90M+ passengers per year, took a direct hit. The gap between Iran's diplomatic statements and its operational actions is now confirmed. Gulf state governments — who had begun cautiously accepting Tehran's gesture — now face a politically impossible position. Trump immediately declared Iran had "surrendered to its neighbours" based on the statement — a framing Iran's IRGC publicly rejected.
● Escalation · United Kingdom
Britain's Entry Changes the Coalition Calculus
A US B-1 Lancer bomber landed at RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire on Friday evening. Prime Minister Keir Starmer granted permission for what Downing Street framed as "defensive US action" against Iranian missile sites — under significant political pressure from Trump and domestic opposition leader Kemi Badenoch, who had criticised the delay. Britain is now operationally in the war. France, Spain, and Italy have provided various levels of defensive military support to Gulf allies. Iran's deputy foreign minister has placed all of them on notice: European nations participating "will become legitimate targets." Cyprus specifically: Turkey is now considering deploying F-16s for protection of the island. The war's geographic footprint is expanding north and west.
● Trump · Expansion Signal
"Areas Not Previously Considered for Targeting"
Trump posted on Truth Social overnight: "Under serious consideration for complete destruction and certain death, because of Iran's bad behavior, are areas and groups of people that were not considered for targeting up until this moment in time." He did not specify what those areas or groups are. Intelligence analysts are reading this in three possible directions: expanded strikes against civilian infrastructure inside Iran, strikes on IRGC-aligned forces in Iraq or Syria, or escalation against Iranian proxies in Yemen. Iran's foreign minister simultaneously rejected any ceasefire or new talks with the Trump administration, and said Tehran is prepared for a US ground invasion. Ali Larijani added that Iranian forces are "waiting" for that scenario and would capture and kill "thousands of US troops."
● Energy · Supply Shock
JPMorgan: Six Million Barrels Per Day Could Vanish Next Week
JPMorgan's head of global commodities research warned clients Friday that production cuts could approach 6 million barrels per day by end of next week if the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. Iraq has already shut 1.5M bpd. Kuwait is cutting after storage filled. UAE is expected to show supply constraints next week. Qatar's energy minister told the Financial Times Gulf exports could halt entirely "within weeks" if the war continues — and that oil could reach $150/barrel, "bring down the economies of the world." The US created a $20B insurance program for oil tankers in the Gulf — it did nothing to calm markets. Morgan Stanley revised its 2026 Brent forecast upward, but the market has already blown past their revised numbers. Brent is currently near $90. The next threshold traders are watching is $100.
● Intelligence · Russia
Russia: Not a Bystander
Russia confirmed the war has created "significant demand" for its energy exports — framing the conflict as a commercial windfall. This follows earlier confirmation that Russian intelligence services are feeding Iran real-time data on US warship and aircraft locations. Putin spoke with Iranian President Pezeshkian, expressing condolences and receiving a full operational briefing. This is not neutrality. India received a 30-day waiver from US sanctions to continue Russian oil imports — signalling Washington is managing India's alignment carefully, aware that New Delhi refusing Russian crude would cause its own supply crisis. The India waiver is a sign the US knows the oil shock is already severe enough to require exemptions for friendly importers.

Global South · Regional Impact Update

Brent near $90. Hormuz near-closed. No ceasefire visible. This is how Day 8 reads across the regions that did not start this war and have no vote in how it ends.

🌊
Caribbean Region
🇹🇹
Trinidad & Tobago
The region's only net beneficiary at current prices. LNG export revenues climbing. But Trinidad's refinery closure in 2018 means it still imports refined petroleum products — so domestic fuel prices are rising even as export revenue grows. The paradox: T&T profits from the crisis in US dollars and pays for it in TTD at the pump.
Net beneficiary
🇬🇾
Guyana
ExxonMobil's Stabroek block producing ~700K bpd. Every dollar on Brent adds directly to Guyana's Natural Resource Fund. The country that was the hemisphere's poorest a decade ago is now its fastest-growing economy — and this crisis is compounding that. Watching for: whether high prices accelerate investment in new offshore blocks or create Dutch disease pressures on non-oil sectors.
Major beneficiary
🇩🇴
Dominican Republic
60%+ of energy runs on hydrocarbons. 41% electricity system losses — meaning every oil price increase hits harder and wastes more. Tourism (significant GDP share) and manufacturing free zones both exposed to a stagflating US economy. The DR had strong growth forecasts for 2026 — those numbers are now under pressure from both energy costs and softening US demand.
High exposure
🇯🇲
Jamaica
100% oil import dependent. Tourism (~50% GDP) draws 50% of arrivals from a US economy that just printed −92,000 jobs. Remittances (17.4% GDP) flow 65% from the same stagflating US. Electricity at $0.27/kWh pre-crisis — already 171% of world average. At $90 Brent, the annualised GDP damage estimate sits above $315M. The $100 threshold — which triggers JPS emergency surcharges — is $10 away. No strategic petroleum reserve. No buffer.
Critical exposure
🇧🇧
Barbados
Post-IMF reform trajectory now colliding with an exogenous oil shock. Tourism (~40% GDP) draws heavily from UK visitors — who are now in a country that formally entered the war today. Any European economic slowdown from energy costs compresses UK discretionary travel directly. The IMF program gave Barbados fiscal tools — but those tools were not designed for $90+ oil.
High exposure
🇭🇹
Haiti
The most exposed nation in the hemisphere. 77% electricity grid losses. Active gang control of Port-au-Prince. Fuel imported by truck from Dominican Republic. Any further DR supply disruption or price escalation hits hospitals, water pumps, and food supply simultaneously. Zero fiscal capacity to respond. This is not a macroeconomic story — it is a humanitarian one being buried under coverage of the war itself.
Humanitarian risk
🌍
West Africa
🇳🇬
Nigeria
Africa's largest oil exporter is seeing revenue climb — but the Dangote refinery is only partially online, meaning domestic consumers still pay world prices for refined fuel despite the country sitting on crude. Domestic inflation and pump price pressure rising in parallel with export revenue. The net position is positive for government budgets but painful for households. Watch: whether Abuja uses windfall to rebuild reserves or service political patronage.
Net beneficiary (strained)
🇬🇭
Ghana
Among the most dangerous combinations on the map: active IMF debt restructuring arriving simultaneously with a sustained oil shock. Ghana defaulted in 2022 and is still in recovery. It produces modest crude but imports refined products — so every dollar on Brent widens the fiscal hole the IMF is trying to close. Gold and cocoa exports provide partial offset, but the structural mismatch is severe. The IMF program's conditions become harder to meet as fuel subsidies balloon or pump prices rise.
Critical — debt + oil shock
🇸🇳
Senegal
First oil production year (2024, Sangomar field) positions Senegal as one of the war's unlikely beneficiaries in West Africa. BP/Kosmos LNG development becomes more financially attractive at $90 oil. Short-term: still importing refined fuel and feeling domestic price pressure. Medium-term: high prices accelerate offshore investment and improve revenue projections for a country that desperately needs fiscal space.
Emerging beneficiary
🌏
South Asia
🇮🇳
India
The world's third-largest oil importer absorbs this at scale no other Global South country matches. $15–18B added to the import bill per $10 rise in Brent. India received a 30-day US waiver to continue Russian oil purchases — Washington's acknowledgement that cutting India's Russian supply would be too destabilising. But Hormuz carries 90% of oil flowing to Asian markets. India's non-Russian supply runs through it. The waiver protects one channel. It does not protect the Strait. Fertilizer import exposure is also severe — planting season has begun.
High — scale exposure
🇵🇰
Pakistan
Petrol price raised 20% today to PKR 321/litre. Pakistan is on its third IMF program. Thin foreign reserves. 4M+ workers in Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait — the exact countries under Iranian missile attack. The Gulf remittances sustaining Pakistan's FX position are now geographically inside the conflict zone. LNG prices spiking as Qatar's exports halt. Pakistan's electricity grid is LNG-dependent. This is a multi-vector shock hitting an economy with no cushion.
Critical — multi-vector
🇱🇰
Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's 2022 economic collapse was triggered by a foreign exchange crisis that made fuel unaffordable — queues, blackouts, political revolution. The country is in fragile recovery under IMF supervision. The conditions that caused 2022 are re-emerging: fuel import costs spiking, FX reserves thin, external account under pressure. Additionally: the US Navy sank an Iranian frigate south of Sri Lanka last week. The Indian Ocean is now an active naval theatre adjacent to the island's coastline.
2022 conditions returning
🌴
Southeast Asia
🇵🇭
Philippines
The most underreported exposure in this brief. 2M+ Overseas Filipino Workers in Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait — remittances equal ~10% of Philippine GDP. Those workers are geographically inside the conflict zone. If Gulf economies contract and reduce employment, or if workers evacuate, the Philippines faces a simultaneous oil price shock and remittance collapse. The BPO sector provides partial buffer — but it cannot absorb a 10% GDP hit.
Critical — OFW exposure

Meridian · Day 8 Assessment
Week Two: The War Is Getting Larger, Not Smaller

Day 8 is the day the war structurally widened. Britain entered. Turkey is mobilising. Iran hit Dubai after pledging restraint. Trump is threatening targets "not previously considered." The de-escalation signals that briefly appeared yesterday have inverted. Every indicator points toward escalation continuing through at least Week Three.

For the Global South, the oil number is the number. $90 Brent is already above the critical thresholds for Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Haiti, Ghana, and the Eastern Caribbean. JPMorgan's warning of 6M bpd in additional production cuts by end of next week — if accurate — would push Brent toward $110–$120 in a market that is already structurally short. At that level the Caribbean's economic damage models move from "serious" to "systemic." At $120, Jamaica's alumina refineries become unviable. Sri Lanka's 2022 replay becomes a question of timing, not probability. Ghana's IMF program stalls.

The only structural good news in this brief: Guyana and Trinidad are accumulating revenue windfalls that — if governed well — could fund a decade of infrastructure. The opportunity for Caribbean energy solidarity exists in this moment. Whether regional governments move to exploit it is a political question. The intelligence is clear. The window is open. It will not stay open.
