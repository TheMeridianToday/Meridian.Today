The 15-Point Proposal · Pakistan Channel
US-Iran · March 25 · Diplomatic Track
Sanctions relief.
Nuclear cooperation.
Hormuz access.

The United States has transmitted a 15-point proposal to Iran through Pakistani government intermediaries, two Pakistani officials confirmed to the Associated Press. The plan covers: sanctions relief, civilian nuclear cooperation, rollback of Iran's nuclear program, IAEA monitoring, missile limits, and access for shipping through the Strait of Hormuz [^98^].

Pakistan has offered to host renewed negotiations between Washington and Tehran. The US has agreed "in principle" to talks in Pakistan, though mediators are still working to convince Iran to participate formally [^98^]. The proposal represents the first concrete diplomatic framework since the war began on February 28.

The market responded immediately. Brent crude futures for May delivery dropped to just under $98 per barrel, down from over $101 on Tuesday — a 3% dip on diplomatic optimism. WTI crude futures traded at just over $87 per barrel [^98^].

But the military track continues in parallel. Israel bombed what it described as Iran's "most central explosives production facility" in Isfahan, killing 12 and wounding 28. A fuel tank at Kuwait International Airport burst into flames after a drone strike. Israeli Defense Forces spokesperson stated operations would continue "according to an unchanged plan, regardless of any diplomatic efforts" [^98^].

The Two-Track War
Day 25 reveals the bifurcated nature of the conflict: a diplomatic track seeking resolution and a military track pursuing attrition. The 15-point proposal offers Iran a path to sanctions relief and normalized nuclear activity in exchange for missile limits and Hormuz guarantees. Israel's position is that strikes will continue regardless of talks. For the Global South, the critical insight is that even successful diplomacy will not reverse immediate economic damage. Oil at $98 is still $30 above pre-war levels. Retail fuel prices — sticky due to inventory cycles, taxes, and margins — will not drop for weeks even if crude stabilizes. The proposal is hope. The pump price is reality. Source: Associated Press · Indian Express · NPR
Hormuz · The Non-Hostile Lane
IMO Notification · March 25 · Selective Access
"Non-hostile" vessels
may transit.
US and Israeli-affiliated excluded.

Iran's Foreign Ministry informed the International Maritime Organization on Tuesday that vessels deemed "non-hostile" can transit the Strait of Hormuz safely. In a letter shared with IMO member states, Iran defined non-hostile ships as those that "neither take part in nor assist hostile actions against Iran and are not affiliated with the United States or Israel" [^98^].

The notification is a unilateral declaration, not a negotiated agreement. It formalizes the de facto reality of the past week: Chinese-flagged vessels, Indian vessels under yuan-clearance, and select European neutrals have transited while US and Israeli-affiliated shipping remains excluded. Six vessels transited on March 24, up from near-standstill levels, but negligible against the 138 daily average pre-war [^56^].

For the Global South, the "non-hostile" designation creates a two-tier shipping market. Vessels with US or Israeli insurance, flag, or cargo face continued exclusion. Vessels willing to declare neutrality — or operate under yuan-clearance — may pass. The insurance market remains frozen for most operators. The cost of compliance is a 30-40% premium over pre-war rates.

Kenya · The Supply Chain Breaks
East Africa · March 25 · Price Freeze Collapse
20% of outlets
already dry.
Dealers threaten national halt.

Kenya's fuel crisis has reached criticality. The Petroleum Outlets Association of Kenya confirmed that 20% of the country's 3,100 independent fuel retailers are running short of supplies. Chairman Martin Chomba warned: "In two weeks it will be a total crisis with no fuel in most outlets if the tension in the Middle East continues" [^97^].

The crisis stems from the March 14 decision by the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority to freeze pump prices for 30 days despite surging global costs. The freeze was calculated on cargoes received February 10-March 9 — pre-war pricing. With Brent averaging $95 in March versus $69 in February, dealers are selling at a loss [^92^].

Energy Minister Opiyo Wandayi attempted reassurance Wednesday: "The country had sufficient stock and urged consumers not to panic buy or hoard" [^94^]. But the stock is not reaching retailers. The association — representing 68% of the national market — is pressuring authorities to allow private supplier sourcing. The risk is diversion to unregulated neighbors offering better margins [^97^].

The price freeze is breaking the supply chain. What was designed as consumer protection is becoming supply cessation. The next EPRA review is April 15. The dealers' timeline is immediate.

Nigeria · The Crude-Retail Divide
West Africa · March 25 · Price Rigidity
Crude drops to $100.
Retail stays at N1,350.
The lag hurts.

Despite crude oil prices dropping to $100 per barrel from $130 peak, Nigerian retail petrol prices remain stuck at N1,300-1,350 per litre. Marketers have not adjusted pump prices to reflect the global softening [^103^].

The Dangote Refinery — which has implemented five price hikes in March alone — last raised its gantry price to N1,275 per litre on March 21. Coastal prices reached N1,646,748 per metric tonne. The refinery's diesel price jumped to N1,750 per litre, up N250 from N1,500 [^95^][^96^].

The stickiness has structural causes. Nigeria's deregulated market transmits global volatility instantly upward but resists downward adjustment due to inventory cycles, margin protection, and currency risk. The naira trades at N1,650 to the dollar. Even at $100 crude, the import parity price — what Nigeria would pay if it imported refined products — remains elevated due to freight costs and the 40% premium on Gulf shipping [^99^].

The IEA's "biggest supply disruption in history" has created a one-way ratchet. Prices rise immediately on crude spikes. They do not fall correspondingly when crude drops. The Global South absorbs the asymmetry.

Caribbean · 23 Days of Silence
Regional Response · March 2 vs. March 25
Barbados capped VAT.
Jamaica hiked prices.
No coordination.

Twenty-three days have passed since CARICOM's last coordinated statements on March 2. Since then, individual nations have implemented fragmented responses without regional framework:

Barbados — Locked in heavy fuel oil price at $92/barrel for three months through hedging. Capped VAT on gasoline at 47 cents/litre and diesel at 37 cents/litre until March 31, 2027. Retail prices: gasoline $3.79/litre, diesel $3.27/litre [^109^][^114^].

Jamaica — Petrojam implemented four price increases in March. 90-octane reached $175.32/litre on March 25. No hedging announced. No VAT relief. Prices track global market directly [^115^][^116^].

Grenada — March 18 increases: gasoline $15.18 EC/gallon, diesel $16.11 EC/gallon, 100lb cooking gas cylinder $296.60 EC. Ministry monitoring $17 EC/gallon threshold for intervention [^45^].

The fragmentation is the point. Each nation responds alone. The windfall from Trinidad & Tobago and Guyana oil production — both nations pumping at record rates — remains undeployed for regional stabilization. No CARICOM energy security framework has been activated. No coordinated strategic reserve. No joint procurement. The 15-point US-Iran proposal does not mention Caribbean interests. The silence continues.
