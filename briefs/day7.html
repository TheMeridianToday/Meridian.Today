Operation Epic Fury · Day 7

Israel Claims Near-Complete Air Superiority. The IRGC Fires Its 23rd Wave.

Israel's military announced it has carried out 2,500 strikes across Iran, destroying 80% of Iran's air defence systems and claiming "near-complete air superiority" over Iranian airspace. Friday saw a fresh "broad wave" of strikes targeting Tehran's government infrastructure and Hezbollah strongholds in Beirut's southern suburbs simultaneously. The US military said Iranian targets are "being decimated" and that Iran's ballistic missile attacks have fallen 90% from Day 1 levels while drone attacks dropped 83%.

Iran's answer: the IRGC announced its 23rd wave of strikes — missiles and drones continuing toward Israel, Gulf Arab states, and US-linked infrastructure. Iran has now fired over 500 ballistic and naval missiles and nearly 2,000 drones since February 28. Approximately 40% toward Israel, 60% toward US targets in the region. On Friday, Iranian debris started fires in central Israel. Eight Israeli soldiers were wounded by Hezbollah fire.

⟁ New Development · Geopolitical Escalation
Russia is providing Iran with real-time intelligence on the locations of American warships and aircraft. Multiple US intelligence sources confirmed this on March 6. Every carrier group, every destroyer escort, every sortie operating in the Gulf and beyond — Russia knows it and is transmitting coordinates to a nation in active hostilities with the United States. This is not neutrality. This is the new strategic architecture of this war, confirmed in writing, on record, on Day 7.

The CIA is confirmed to be arming Iranian Kurdish forces — Clarissa Ward reporting on the ground in Kurdistan for CNN — with the explicit aim of fomenting a popular uprising inside Iran. A Kurdish military base in Iraq's Kurdistan was simultaneously struck by Iranian missiles in what appeared to be a counter-operation. The war inside the war has now officially begun.

Lebanon's crisis deepened: Israel ordered the evacuation of all of Dahiyeh — Beirut's southern suburbs, home to hundreds of thousands — then struck heavily overnight. Lebanon's Prime Minister warned "a humanitarian disaster is looming." The UN rights office warned the displacement orders affecting over 100 towns risk constituting "prohibited forced displacement" under international law. Ghana's UN peacekeepers in southern Lebanon reported two soldiers critically injured by two missile strikes within 10 minutes.

Oil · Markets · Week One Close

The Week Oil Rewrote Its Own History. What Comes Next Is Not In Any Model.

Let the closing numbers speak first. WTI crude: $90.90 — up 12.21% on Friday alone, up 35.63% for the week. That is the largest weekly gain in US crude oil futures history, a market that has been trading since 1983. Brent: $92.69 — up 8.52% Friday, up 28% for the week. Brent's weekly move is the biggest since April 2020. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is at a near-total halt. Oil storage tanks across the Gulf are filling up because exports have nearly stopped. The market is not pricing in fear. It is pricing in physical unavailability.

"Oil could easily sprint above $100, onwards to $200 — and bring down the economies of the world."
— Qatar Energy Minister Saad al-Kaabi, Financial Times, March 6, 2026

The supply disruption is now operational, not theoretical. Iraq has shut down 1.5 million barrels per day of production. Kuwait has begun cutting production — it ran out of storage space. JPMorgan's Natasha Kaneva noted: "The market is shifting from pricing pure geopolitical risk to grappling with tangible operational disruption." The Qatar Energy Minister warned that all Gulf exporters will need to declare force majeure if the situation continues — meaning they formally notify the world they cannot fulfil their contracts. Trump announced a $20 billion insurance program for oil tankers in the Persian Gulf. The market barely noticed it and kept climbing.

US Economy · Stagflation Signal
◆
Jobs · Federal Reserve · Risk

Jobs Go Negative. Oil Hits $90. The Federal Reserve Has No Answer For This.

February's US payrolls came in at −92,000 jobs — the first negative jobs report since 2020. It landed on the same afternoon oil hit $90 per barrel for the first time since 2023. Traders and analysts immediately named what they were seeing: stagflation — the combination of a stagnating economy and rising prices that the Federal Reserve cannot treat with a single tool. Cutting rates to fight the job losses pumps more money into an inflation environment already driven by oil. Raising rates to fight the oil-driven inflation crushes the already-weakening jobs market. The Fed is trapped.

Jamaica + Caribbean · Direct Impact
A stagflating US economy directly compresses Jamaican economic lifelines. Remittances ($3.5B, 17.4% of GDP): US consumers under financial stress send less money home. Tourism ($4.3B, ~50% of GDP): American discretionary travel spending is the first thing cut when paycheques shrink and fuel costs rise. Both channels — the two pillars of the Jamaican economy — are now under simultaneous pressure. This was not in the original 90-day model. It is additive to the oil damage already being calculated.

Goldman Sachs said a temporary surge to $100 per barrel could slow global growth by 0.4 percentage points. At $120 — where Barclays and UBS analysts say we could be headed if Hormuz stays closed — the growth reduction is materially larger. For small, open, tourism-and-remittance-dependent economies like Jamaica, Trinidad, Barbados, and Guyana, those fractions of global GDP translate into full percentage points of local contraction.

Meridian · Week One Verdict · March 6, 2026

THE PRICE OF
WEEK ONE.

Seven days. No exit. No off-ramp. And the biggest oil week since 1983.

The closing number that will define this week in history books is not the death toll, though 1,332 killed and 181 children should be enough to define any week. It is not Trump's "unconditional surrender" declaration, though that phrase has no precedent in modern American foreign policy. The number is 35.63%. That is how much US crude oil rose in seven days — a record that stretches back to the birth of futures trading in 1983. Nothing in Iraq, nothing in Libya, nothing in the Gulf wars produced a weekly oil move like this. Markets are not pricing in a war. They are pricing in a structural break in global energy supply.

Russia's confirmed involvement changes the map. When a nuclear-armed permanent member of the UN Security Council begins feeding targeting intelligence to a nation in active combat with the United States, the war is no longer between three countries. It is a proxy contest between two global powers — and the stakes are no longer limited to Iranian regime change. Every US ship in the Gulf is now navigating waters where Russia knows its coordinates and Iran has been told where it is. That is the definition of a militarily hot proxy conflict.

For the Global South, for Jamaica, for the Caribbean — the fertilizer shock confirmed this week is the story everyone missed. The Hormuz blockage was always going to hit fuel prices. But the same strait carries 25–35% of the world's traded fertilizer. Iran is the world's third-largest urea exporter. The Northern Hemisphere planting season starts in weeks. If farmers from Kansas to Kenya cannot get fertilizer at planting, the harvest shortfall arrives six months from now — and it arrives on top of oil-elevated food costs already in motion. The Caribbean food inflation scenario we modelled at $140 oil may arrive faster and at lower oil prices than projected, compounded by a fertilizer shock that was not in the original model.

One week down. $7 from $100 oil. Biggest weekly energy move since 1983. A political vacuum in Tehran. A Russia now confirmed in the intelligence war. And an American president whose only public offer is two words. That is the world as of Friday, March 6, 2026. Meridian will be watching.
